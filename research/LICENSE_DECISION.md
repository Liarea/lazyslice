# Licence decision

**Question.** One licence for the lazysnap CLI, and one repo structure, given that the CLI is the whole of v1 and the eventual paid product is a hosted service.

**What is being licensed.** A single static binary a developer runs on a laptop or in CI against their own database ([CONCEPT.md](../CONCEPT.md)). Not a server, not a daemon, not something a customer's users reach over a network; a hosted service is an explicit v1 non-goal. That shape decides most of what follows, because two of the four candidates are designed around software that *is* a network service.

**Method and as-of date.** Researched 4–5 September 2026. Every licence text below was read from its canonical source on 5 September 2026 — [SPDX](https://spdx.org/licenses/BUSL-1.1.html) for BUSL-1.1, [apache.org](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0.txt) for Apache-2.0, [gnu.org](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/agpl-3.0.en.html) for AGPL-3.0, [fsl.software](https://fsl.software/FSL-1.1-ALv2.template.md) for FSL, [developercertificate.org](https://developercertificate.org/) for the DCO — and project `LICENSE` files were read raw from GitHub and GitLab, not from a licence-detection badge. Repository facts (stars, licence, push dates) came from the [GitHub REST API](https://docs.github.com/rest) on 5 September 2026 and will drift, as will vendor blog positions and corporate policy pages. Reddit was not reachable from this environment; developer sentiment is from Hacker News, blogs, vendor posts and issue trackers only. The project was renamed **lazyslice** on 5 September 2026 ([ADR-000](../docs/adr/000-name.md)); where the prose below says "lazysnap" it is quoting the pre-rename name, and every forward-looking recommendation says lazyslice.

## The four candidates

The SPDX identifier for the Business Source License is `BUSL-1.1`; everyone says "BSL". Both appear below, and a compliance reviewer greps for `BUSL-1.1`.

| | Apache-2.0 | MIT | AGPL-3.0 | BUSL-1.1 (BSL) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OSI open source | yes | yes | yes | **no** ([SPDX](https://spdx.org/licenses/BUSL-1.1.html)) |
| Explicit patent grant | yes ([§3](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0)) | no | yes | only via the Change Licence |
| Stops a cloud vendor hosting it | no | no | barely — see below | yes, until the Change Date |
| Eligible for homebrew-core | yes | yes | yes | **no** — core formulae must be [DFSG-compatible](https://docs.brew.sh/Acceptable-Formulae) |
| Blanket-banned at a named large employer | no | no | yes, [at Google](https://opensource.google/documentation/reference/using/agpl-policy) | none found — see gaps |
| Lets *us* shell out to it from a closed service | yes | yes | yes — process boundary, no §13 duty | yes |
| Lets *us* import it into a closed service | yes | yes | only with a CLA or sole ownership | yes |

### Contributor comfort

Apache-2.0 and MIT are what a drive-by contributor to a Go/Rust CLI expects; neither makes them think. AGPL excludes anyone whose employer bans it — Google's policy is "Code licensed under the GNU Affero General Public License (AGPL) MUST NOT be used at Google", and the workstation rule is a default with an exception rather than an absolute: "Do not install AGPL-licensed programs on your workstation, Google-issued laptop, or Google-issued phone without explicit authorization from the Open Source Programs Office" ([Google](https://opensource.google/documentation/reference/using/agpl-policy), last updated 2025-06-10). The exception does not rescue the argument: a contributor who must file with an OSPO before installing the tool is a contributor we have lost, and Google's own stated reason is that it maintains "an aggressively-broad ban on all AGPL software" precisely to keep the question from ever being asked. BSL is the one that actually costs contributions: VictoriaMetrics, who sell a hosted product, argue "Switching an open source license to the BSL erodes trust in your product and your company" and that it is "likely to reduce your pool of contributors considerably" ([VictoriaMetrics](https://victoriametrics.com/blog/bsl-is-short-term-fix-why-we-choose-open-source/)). The empirical version is Terraform: HashiCorp moved MPL-2.0 → BSL 1.1 on 10 August 2023 ([HashiCorp](https://www.hashicorp.com/en/blog/hashicorp-adopts-business-source-license)), the [OpenTofu manifesto](https://opentofu.org/manifesto/) ("the BUSL license is a poison pill for Terraform") followed within two weeks, and by 25 August 2023 the community had forked and filed to join the Linux Foundation ([OpenTofu](https://opentofu.org/blog/opentofu-announces-fork-of-terraform/)).

### Corporate adoption friction

This is the deciding axis: lazysnap's adoption path is one developer, then their team, then a compliance conversation about a tool that touches production data. Apache-2.0 is the lowest-friction option in that setting precisely because it makes the patent grant explicit where MIT is silent, and adds a trademark clause ([§3, §6](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0)). AGPL is where review stalls — not because the licence is unreasonable but because of cost and boundary uncertainty: "Case-by-case legal review of AGPL dependencies across a hundred thousand engineers costs more than any of the software is worth to them" ([Pettus](https://thebuild.com/blog/the-agpl-radioactive-by-design/)). BSL fails earlier and harder, on a rule that is written down rather than inferred: "A formula in homebrew/core must be open source under a licence compatible with the Debian Free Software Guidelines" ([Homebrew](https://docs.brew.sh/Acceptable-Formulae)), and SPDX's own notes on BUSL-1.1 say flatly that it "is not an Open Source license" ([SPDX](https://spdx.org/licenses/BUSL-1.1.html)). That directly contradicts the "one static binary, one-line install" principle in CONCEPT.md — a third-party tap still works ([Homebrew](https://docs.brew.sh/Acceptable-Formulae)), but `brew install lazyslice` with no `brew tap` first does not. Sentry's own case against BSL is that the Additional Use Grant "is a giant fill-in-the-blank that effectively means that every BSL is a different license", forcing compliance teams to "review each case individually" ([Sentry](https://blog.sentry.io/introducing-the-functional-source-license-freedom-without-free-riding/)). Note the asymmetry with the AGPL evidence, and do not overstate it: the homebrew-core exclusion is a published rule naming the criterion, whereas no published corporate policy naming BSL by name was found, unlike Google's AGPL page. The BSL friction argument rests on "fails a policy that requires an OSI-approved licence", which is a common policy shape but not one this document has a named employer for.

### Protection against a cloud vendor hosting the tool

Worth being honest about how weak this threat is *for this product*. The strip-mining fight was about databases and search engines, where the open source project **is** the hosted service — Redis names it directly: "cloud providers reap the profits and control the infrastructure without proportional contributions back" ([Redis](https://redis.io/blog/agplv3/)). A subsetting/masking CLI is not that. The hosted product's moat is scheduling, retention, sharing and audit, none of which is in the CLI.

- **Apache-2.0 / MIT** — no protection. A vendor may host it. Irrelevant until the tool is big enough to be worth hosting, by which point our own hosted layer exists.
- **AGPL-3.0** — far less protection than it looks, and the answer depends on the *boundary*, not on who is standing on the other side of it. §13 opens "Notwithstanding any other provision of this License, **if you modify the Program**, your modified version must prominently offer all users interacting with it remotely through a computer network … an opportunity to receive the Corresponding Source of your version" ([AGPL-3.0 §13](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/agpl-3.0.en.html), emphasis added). So:
  - **(a) A separate process, invoked and left alone.** A control plane that execs an unmodified `lazyslice` binary and reads its output has not modified the Program and has not linked it: "If you run stock upstream AGPL software as part of your service, Section 13 asks nothing of you" ([Pettus](https://thebuild.com/blog/the-agpl-radioactive-by-design/)). The FSF's own test is "the mechanism of communication (exec, pipes, rpc, function calls within a shared address space, etc.) and the semantics of the communication" ([GPL FAQ, #MereAggregation](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-faq.html#MereAggregation)); a simple exec with a config in and rows out sits on the separate-programs side. **This answer is the same for a competitor and for us.** Neither needs a CLA to do it.
  - **(b) Importing the CLI as a Go module, or patching it.** Go links statically, so an import puts our packages and the service in one binary and one address space — "If modules are designed to run linked together in a shared address space, that almost surely means combining them into one program" ([GPL FAQ](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-faq.html#MereAggregation)) — and any patch is a modification on the face of §13. Then the combined work is AGPL and the service's source must be offered to its users. The only ways out are sole copyright ownership or a CLA that grants us rights beyond the outbound licence. This is the case the comparison table's "only with a CLA" row is about, and it is also the shape [ARCHITECTURE-level plans](../docs/BUILD_PLAN.md) make most likely for us, since a hosted layer wanting the planner and the maskers would import them rather than shell out.
  - The verdict stands, but for a sharper reason than "near-zero defence": AGPL costs us every contributor at an AGPL-banning employer, defends nothing against a vendor who chooses architecture (a), and constrains **us** in exactly the architecture (b) we would most likely build. It is the one option whose costs and whose restrictions both land on our side.
- **BUSL-1.1 (BSL)** — real protection: no production use unless we write an Additional Use Grant. The frequently-repeated claim that a BSL project must eventually become copyleft is **false**, and this document previously asserted it. The covenant reads: "To specify as the Change License the GPL Version 2.0 or any later version, **or a license that is compatible with GPL Version 2.0 or a later version, where 'compatible' means that software provided under the Change License can be included in a program with software provided under GPL Version 2.0 or a later version**" ([SPDX](https://spdx.org/licenses/BUSL-1.1.html)). MIT and Apache-2.0 both satisfy that, and a permissive Change Licence is ordinary practice: Materialize's `LICENSE` reads "Change License: Apache License, Version 2.0" ([Materialize](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MaterializeInc/materialize/main/LICENSE)) and Terraform's reads "Change License: MPL 2.0" ([Terraform](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hashicorp/terraform/main/LICENSE)). FSL's own FAQ confirms it from the other direction: asked whether FSL can take a different change licence, it answers "No … Can you try to use the Business Source License, though? It's similar to FSL but allows for any change license" ([FSL](https://fsl.software/)). **The reasons to reject BSL are the three that survive:** it is not an Open Source licence by SPDX's own note; that makes it ineligible for homebrew-core ([Homebrew](https://docs.brew.sh/Acceptable-Formulae)); and the Additional Use Grant is a per-project variable, so "every BSL is a different license" and each one must be reviewed individually ([Sentry](https://blog.sentry.io/introducing-the-functional-source-license-freedom-without-free-riding/)) — visible in the wild as Materialize's memory-and-disk-limit grant versus Terraform's competitive-offering grant, two unrelated tests under one licence name.
- **If we ever conclude we need a source-available core, [FSL 1.1](https://fsl.software/) is still the better instrument** — but on its real advantages, not on a copyleft difference that does not exist. FSL is a standardised text with no fill-in-the-blank: the Competing Use test is written into the licence rather than authored per project, which is the exact defect FSL names in BSL ("the Additional Use Grant is a serious flaw, because it creates too much variability. Each BSL implementation is essentially a new license. Also, four years is too long", [FSL](https://fsl.software/)). Its conversion clause is an irrevocable grant on a two-year clock rather than a four-year cap we would have to opt below: "We hereby irrevocably grant you an additional license to use the Software under the Apache License, Version 2.0 that is effective on the second anniversary of the date we make the Software available" ([FSL-1.1-ALv2](https://fsl.software/FSL-1.1-ALv2.template.md)). Neither is an Open Source licence, so both lose homebrew-core; FSL loses less of everything else.

## A future closed hosted layer: same repo or separate

**Same repo, permissive root + proprietary subdirectory** is the GitLab pattern — a root LICENSE saying "All content that resides under the 'ee/' directory of this repository, if that directory exists, is licensed under the license defined in 'ee/LICENSE'" ([GitLab](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab/-/raw/master/LICENSE)), where `ee/LICENSE` permits production use only with "a valid GitLab Enterprise Edition subscription" ([ee/LICENSE](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab/-/raw/master/ee/LICENSE)). Neosync copied the wording verbatim and never used it. It works, but it costs:

- **Licence detection breaks.** GitHub reports Neosync as `NOASSERTION` because Licensee cannot match a modified file; GitHub's advice is to "simplify your LICENSE file and note the complexity somewhere else" ([GitHub](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/licensing-a-repository)). A scanner-driven procurement review then flags us as unlicensed — the exact audience we need to pass.
- **Every contributor must reason about which directory they are in**, and a good-faith PR into the wrong one becomes a legal problem rather than a review comment.
- It is premature regardless: there is no hosted code to place.

**Separate repos** cost one decision — extract the reusable parts as a permissively licensed module the private service imports — and nothing else. The public repo keeps one licence, one file, correctly detected.

By licence, if the hosted layer lands: **Apache-2.0/MIT core** is trivial in either layout, closed service included. **AGPL core** makes *our own* service the problem, not a competitor's; keeping it closed needs a CLA from every contributor. **BSL core** works in one repo but the community core is already gone.

## CLA or DCO

A CLA's real function is retained relicensing power: MongoDB in 2019 and Elasticsearch in January 2021 both "used these rights granted by its CLA to achieve a move to a non-open-source license" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contributor_License_Agreement)). Both later reversed course — Elastic added AGPL in August 2024, conceding "We had issues with AWS and the market confusion their offering was causing" ([Elastic](https://www.elastic.co/blog/elasticsearch-is-open-source-again)) — and Redis added AGPLv3 in May 2025 because SSPL "hurt our relationship with the Redis community" ([Redis](https://redis.io/blog/agplv3/)). That is the clearest available evidence that the licence-tightening play does not pay for itself.

The [DCO 1.1](https://developercertificate.org/) is only a signed attestation, clauses (a)–(d), that the contributor had the right to submit; it grants no relicensing power. Note the honest counter-argument: Kyle Mitchell holds that the DCO assumes "the project is GPL, and specifically GPLv2" and that using it without further legal scaffolding "achieves next to nothing", giving "the illusion of protection" ([Mitchell](https://writing.kemitchell.com/2021/07/02/DCO-Not-CLA)). That critique bites for a project that expects to enforce or relicense. We expect neither, and the CNCF — which does run large multi-vendor projects — requires exactly this: "All new inbound code contributions to the CNCF shall be (i) accompanied by a Developer Certificate of Origin sign-off" ([CNCF charter §11](https://github.com/cncf/foundation/blob/main/charter.md)). The cost of a CLA is paid at the first contribution: a typo fix needs a signing workflow, and a corporate CLA drags in the contributor's legal team.

We do not want relicensing power. Wanting it is what produces the Terraform outcome.

## Recommendation

1. **Apache-2.0 for the CLI.** Permissive so it spreads without a legal conversation; Apache over MIT because the explicit patent grant and trademark clause ([§3, §6](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0)) are what a compliance reviewer asks for, and because Greenmask — the nearest comparable — is already there. Unmodified `LICENSE` at the repo root, plus a `NOTICE` file.
2. **One public repo, one licence, no `ee/` directory.** The hosted layer, when it exists, goes in a separate private repo importing the CLI as a module. Revisit only if we ship a paid *local* feature — and even then prefer a second repo over a carve-out.
3. **DCO, not a CLA.** `git commit -s` plus the DCO check on PRs, and a line in `CONTRIBUTING.md` saying the project will not be relicensed. The DCO's weakness as a legal instrument is acceptable precisely because we are giving up the power a CLA would preserve.
4. **Protect the name, not the code.** Publish a trademark policy for "lazysnap", the standard defence for a permissive project and the thing Apache §6 already gestures at. A vendor may host the code; they may not call it lazysnap.
5. **Escape hatch, decided now rather than in a panic.** If a vendor ever hosts lazysnap at scale, the answer is [FSL 1.1](https://fsl.software/) (Apache-2.0 after two years) on *new* hosted-adjacent components in a separate repo — never a relicence of the shipped CLI. Without a CLA we could not relicence it anyway, which is the point.
